The Price of a Conscience

April 29, 2026

Anthropic said no. The Pentagon wanted AI free of guardrails. No restrictions on domestic mass surveillance. No limits on autonomous weapons. Anthropic refused to remove the safety rails. So the DoD called them a “supply chain risk,” sued, and lost.

And then Google signed the deal anyway.

This story keeps me up at night. Not because I’m surprised. Because it reveals something ugly about how this industry works. When one company draws a line in the sand, another will happily sell the shovel to dig it up. OpenAI did it. xAI did it. Now Google, despite 950 employees signing an open letter pleading with leadership to follow Anthropic’s lead.

Google’s contract includes language about not intending the AI to be used for mass surveillance or autonomous weapons. But nobody can tell me those words are enforceable. They’re a fig leaf. A sentence that sounds good in a press release and means nothing in a classified briefing room.

Here’s the truth: principles that only hold until someone offers more money were never principles. They were pricing strategies.

I don’t say this to shame any one company. I say it because the pattern matters. Every time a principled refusal gets bypassed by a willing replacement, the next refusal gets harder. The benchmark shifts. We normalize the thing we said we wouldn’t do.

Saying no matters even when someone else says yes. Maybe especially then.
